# Calibrate rain and wind before chasing radar

Beyond a blend of global models and per-station bias correction, the measurable gains for a 0–7 day US point forecast come from four places. The first is **better first-moment correction that keeps working out to day 7**: decaying-average and Kalman-filter bias removal cut 2 m temperature RMSE by 10% to over 70% in one ECMWF study. The second is **calibrating the forecast distribution**, not just its centre: EMOS-type spread calibration and properly modelled rain probability, with gust post-processing improving CRPS by up to 40%. The third is **adding skilled, partly independent inputs**: NBM, HRRR, and AI models such as AIFS and AIGFS, which match or beat ECMWF IFS on 2 m temperature but are weaker on short-range rain. The fourth is **a fast-fading correction from the latest observation**, which pays off only in the first few hours. Real-time observations, upwind stations and radar are the most intuitive ideas, and they have the shortest reach. NWS LAMP, which is MOS updated hourly with observations, "handily outperforms MOS in the 1–12 hour projections" and then converges back to MOS ([Rudack 2008](https://www.weather.gov/media/mdl/Rudack2008.pdf)). Radar extrapolation beats NWP only for about the first 2–2.5 hours ([Imhoff et al. 2023](https://pure.tudelft.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/151585350/Quart_J_Royal_Meteoro_Soc_2023_Imhoff_Scale_dependent_blending_of_ensemble_rainfall_nowcasts_and_numerical_weather.pdf)). Weather-pattern ("regime") effects such as wind direction, pressure systems and terrain do produce real, flow-dependent errors. But they are best captured as predictors inside a regression, and good analog methods need 12–15 months of paired forecasts and observations ([Delle Monache et al. 2013](https://opensky.library.ucar.edu/islandora/object/articles%3A12922)). For an app that has just built a 365-day archive, a regime correction, an observation nudge, a terrain ring, five new models and a backtester, the highest-value next step is **logistic calibration of rain probability**, followed by **speed-dependent wind correction** and **per-site fitting of the observation-fade time**. Radar nowcasting and a cross-station ML model should come last.

## Observations and radar help for hours, not days

The evidence draws a consistent boundary. In operational US practice, observations sharpen a good model forecast for about 12 hours at most, and the strongest effect is in the first few. LAMP is the cleanest test: it outperforms persistence at every lead, beats MOS clearly over 1–12 h, and then "the decreased predictive value of the observations at the later projections causes the LAMP skill level to diminish and converge to the skill level of MOS" ([Rudack 2008](https://www.weather.gov/media/mdl/Rudack2008.pdf)). Persistence itself fades even faster. LAMP and persistence are "essentially of equal skill at the 1-h projection, and persistence deteriorates rather rapidly" after that ([Glahn 2004](https://weather.gov/media/mdl/Glahn2004NewandImproved.pdf)). By day 1 the contest is over: ECMWF HRES beat persistence for 2 m temperature and 10 m wind at **every lead from 24 to 120 h** ([EGU2019-17155](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2019/EGU2019-17155.pdf?EGUsphere)).

The best-documented recipe for the last-observation correction comes from the Finnish Meteorological Institute's open-source code. It computes the model error at the latest observed hour for temperature, humidity and wind speed, subtracts it at +1 h, and applies it to +2 to +5 h with weights "loosely based on 1 year of model forecast error information" ([fmidev/nowcasting_biascorrection](https://github.com/fmidev/nowcasting_biascorrection)). Note that the source publishes no verification numbers. The app's correction of the next 12 hours, with roughly 4 h e-folding for temperature and 2 h for wind, is the right shape. No source gives canonical fade times, though, and FMI's approach implies they should be fitted from each site's own error autocorrelation rather than fixed.

Two effects are easy to conflate here. The large published RMSE gains (−10% to −70% on ECMWF 2 m temperature) come from a Kalman filter that mostly **removes systematic bias**, which helps at all leads ([IDL Lisbon](https://idlcc.fc.ul.pt/publications.php?id=220)). The error carried forward from the latest observation is short-lived. Google's MetNet-3 shows the same pattern in machine learning: when station values are fed in at run time, results "improve further especially in the early lead times" ([MetNet-3, arXiv 2306.06079](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06079)).

### Upwind stations are mostly already in the model

Reading the stations the weather is coming from is physically sound, but the global and rapid-update models already do it. ECMWF's observation-impact statistics attribute about **26% of 24 h forecast-error reduction to surface-based observations**, and "the largest impact is generally found close to, or just downwind of, observing stations" ([ECMWF](https://www.ecmwf.int/node/23158)). In other words, the upwind signal is already used, and its benefit lands downwind exactly as intended. HRRR assimilates every hour and directly assimilates radar reflectivity through a 36-member ensemble Kalman filter ([NOAA EMC HRRR](https://emc.ncep.noaa.gov/emc/pages/numerical_forecast_systems/hrrr.php)). No source found in this research shows that upwind-station predictors add skill over a modern model-plus-MOS system after about 12 h. The independent value of an upwind reading is therefore confined to two cases. One is the hour or two between a model run's data cutoff and its availability, for example a front or thunderstorm outflow crossing a station 50–100 km upwind. The other is describing the local exposure effects that the model's grid cannot resolve.

### Radar beats models only for the first one to two hours

Extrapolating radar echoes "quickly loses skill after 2 hours, or even less for convective rainfall events or small radar domains." Blending with NWP extends useful lead time to "6 hours or more" ([EGU22-7026](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU22/EGU22-7026.html)). In the Netherlands the nowcast had lower error than the weather model for the first 2–2.5 h. A scale-dependent blend followed the nowcast for 1–2 h, then shifted to the model, and was "better or on par with the best-performing individual product" ([Imhoff et al. 2023](https://pure.tudelft.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/151585350/Quart_J_Royal_Meteoro_Soc_2023_Imhoff_Scale_dependent_blending_of_ensemble_rainfall_nowcasts_and_numerical_weather.pdf)).

Deep-learning nowcasters push the horizon only slightly. DeepMind's DGMR runs to 90 minutes, and an independent Italian test found it the least accurate model over the first 25 minutes, though its error grows slowly ([IRENE, arXiv 2609.17175](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17175)). NowcastNet reaches 3 h, but by then has "a realistic amount of detail, but not in the correct spatial placement" ([arXiv 2407.11317](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.11317)). MetNet-3 claims to beat HRRR and the ECMWF ensemble over CONUS to 24 h, but it has not been released and its claims are unverified ([arXiv 2306.06079](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06079)). There is also a US-specific reason radar adds less here than in European studies: HRRR assimilates radar hourly and issues 15-minute output ([NWS TIN 14-32](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/tins/tin14-32hrrr_aaa.pdf)), so its first two hours are already radar-informed. That is an inference, and no US crossover study was found.

| Technique | Helps most | Fades to near zero by | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radar extrapolation (rain timing) | 0–1 h (to ~2 h for steady rain) | ~2–2.5 h; blend useful to ~6 h | European studies; no US crossover found |
| Latest-observation nudge (temperature, wind) | 0–3 h | ~6–12 h | LAMP convergence; FMI practice; fade times not published |
| Upwind-station reading | 0–3 h, in the gap before the next model run | Next model cycle | Inference; models already assimilate these stations |
| Systematic bias correction (decaying average, Kalman filter) | All leads | Persists to day 5–7 | Strong (NAEFS, Kalman filter studies) |
| Regime/analog correction | All leads, when errors depend on flow | Persists | Moderate; needs ~1 year of data |
| Distribution and PoP calibration | All leads | Persists | Strong for EMOS and gusts; PoP gain not quantified here |

## Weather patterns shift model errors, but the evidence favours predictors over bins

Model errors do depend on the pattern. Delle Monache's analog ensemble finds past forecasts that resemble today's and uses what was actually observed. Built on Environment Canada's GEM model for 10 m wind and 2 m temperature at **550 CONUS stations**, it showed "the ability to capture the flow-dependent behavior of errors." Its skill was equal to or better than logistic regression and it was cheaper to run than EMOS, but it needed **12–15 months of training** ([Delle Monache et al. 2013](https://opensky.library.ucar.edu/islandora/object/articles%3A12922)). Terrain sets the sign of the bias as much as the flow does. "In many cases, mountain valleys are too warm, whereas peaks are too cold" ([DOE EESM](https://eesm.science.energy.gov/presentations/are-atmospheric-models-too-cold-mountains)). Cold biases in maximum temperature over snow-covered complex terrain cluster in November–April and are not explained by snow cover alone ([CU Boulder](https://vivo.colorado.edu/display/pubid_506021)). In a terrain-aware correction, the largest improvements came at stations sitting above the model's smoothed terrain height ([JAMS 2023](https://doaj.org/article/e81ac5fcd47c4f36a6f2428f8fc36639)). That supports the app's 25/80/160 km elevation ring as a source of features.

Wind matters for its strength as well as its direction. HRRR wind errors were negatively correlated with observed speed: it over-forecast light winds and under-forecast strong ones ([Fovell & Gallagher 2022](https://www.atmos.albany.edu/facstaff/rfovell/papers/2022-fovell-gallagher.pdf)). A New York State Mesonet study found **10 m wind over-forecast at all ranges and seasons** by GFS, NAM and HRRR, and all three models warm-biased above 30 °C ([Weather and Forecasting 39(2)](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10488235-verification-global-forecast-system-north-american-mesoscale-forecast-system-high-resolution-rapid-refresh-model-near-surface-forecasts-use-new-york-state-mesonet)). These are regime effects the app can measure directly with its archive.

The specific scenarios in the question have no direct quantitative source in these notes. Those scenarios are storms arriving from the east or west, flow descending from mountains, onshore flow from the sea, and a high or low pressure centre nearby or about 100 miles (160 km) away. What follows is reasoning, not evidence. A pressure centre 160 km away sits well within what a 0.25° (about 28 km) global model resolves, so the system's position is usually forecast correctly at days 1–3. What the model misses is the **local response**. Under a nearby high, clear and calm nights build valley inversions and minimum temperatures come out too warm. Flow coming down a mountain range brings downslope warming, gusts and a rain shadow. Onshore flow brings marine-layer cloud, which caps maximum temperature. Desert monsoon cloud and outflows lead to maximum temperatures that are too high. The app's regime variables are wind direction, pressure anomaly, 24 h pressure change and season, and they are reasonable proxies for these situations. The terrain ring tells the app which direction is "downslope" or "from the sea" at each site.

The research also found a caution: hard binning by wind sector splits a few months of data into tiny samples. Quantile regression forests beat EMOS by adding "any predictor available including statistics on other variables" ([Taillardat et al. 2016](https://hal-meteofrance.archives-ouvertes.fr/meteo-03544106v1)), which is a way to condition on regime implicitly. The app's gate (a regime correction is used only when blocked cross-validation beats plain correction by at least 2%) is the right protection. Given the 12–15 month data requirement for analogs, expect the gate to reject many variables until the archive matures.

## Similar-location pooling works, but needs more sites than the app shows

Pooling across stations is a recurring theme in the literature. Standardized-anomaly MOS (SAMOS) post-processes many stations jointly and achieves skill "comparable to forecasts post-processed individually at every station, and even better on average" ([Univ. Innsbruck WP 2016-08](https://www2.uibk.ac.at/downloads/c4041030/wpaper/2016-08.pdf)). Local Bayesian model averaging works because of "nearby stations that share similar characteristics" ([Kleiber, NCAR](https://www.image.ucar.edu/Seminars/2010/Kleiber_Feb10.shtml)). Correction parameters for sites without observations can be interpolated from neighbours, using altitude as a covariate ([Scheuerer, NOAA PSL](https://psl.noaa.gov/seminars/Abstracts/2013/scheuerer2013.html)). For precipitation, Scheuerer and Hamill found that calibration "crucially relies on the availability of a sufficiently large training dataset", and that pooling across grid points partly compensates for short records ([NOAA repository](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/14608)).

The app has deferred pooling because it has too few sites. One inference worth testing is that **training sites need not be display sites**. Hundreds of ASOS stations have free observation archives, and the Open-Meteo Previous Runs API holds model forecasts back to January 2024 for most models ([Previous Runs docs source](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/previous-runs-api/+page.svelte)). A pooled model could be trained on dozens of similar desert, coastal, mountain and valley stations, described by the same terrain-ring features, and then applied to the app's handful of locations. The free-tier quota (600 calls/minute, 5,000/hour, 10,000/day, with weight rising with variables × models × days) makes this a gradual backfill, not a one-shot job ([api-rate-limiting.md](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo/blob/main/docs/api-rate-limiting.md)).

## AI models and NBM earn their place for temperature, not short-range rain

By late 2026 the AI models are operational and free. On 2 m temperature they are at least as good as the physics models. ECMWF says AIFS Single and AIFS ENS "show superior skill for most upper-air and surface parameters" compared with IFS ([ECMWF](https://www.ecmwf.int/sites/default/files/elibrary/102025/81687-aifs-ens-becomes-operational.pdf)). An independent check at 117 Inner Mongolia stations found AIFS and the WeatherNext 2 ensemble mean both beat IFS-Control on 2 m temperature MAE at every lead from 6 to 84 h ([MDPI Atmosphere 17(11):1053](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4433/17/11/1053)). Precipitation is the weak spot. In the extratropics, which covers the whole continental US, AIFS is less skilful than IFS for precipitation at short ranges and better at long ranges ([Lang et al., arXiv 2406.01465](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.01465)). AIFS ENS underpredicts precipitation more than IFS ENS ([ECMWF Newsletter 185](https://www.ecmwf.int/node/29518)), although a 2025 update reports "significantly improved" 24 h precipitation ([arXiv 2509.18994](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.18994)).

NOAA's AIGFS shows improved large-scale skill over GFS. Its HGEFS, a 62-member mix of physics and AI ensembles, "consistently outperforms both GEFS and AIGEFS across most major verification metrics" ([NOAA VLab abstract](https://vlab.noaa.gov/documents/10157/138440/Abstract_VLabWebinar_Oct2025_AIGFSGEFS.pdf)). That is the best operational evidence that blending AI and physics models beats either alone.

The practical limit is resolution. On Open-Meteo, AIFS Single and AIGFS are **0.25°, 6-hourly** ([ECMWF API docs source](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/ecmwf-api/+page.svelte); [GFS API docs source](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/gfs-api/+page.svelte)). They therefore add no terrain detail, and they smooth daily highs and lows unless bias-corrected. Their Previous Runs coverage is narrower: the AIGFS module stores only temperature, precipitation, pressure, cloud and 10 m wind ([GfsGraphCastVariable.swift](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo/blob/main/Sources/App/GfsGraphCast/GfsGraphCastVariable.swift)). WeatherNext 2 stores no 10 m wind at all ([WeatherNextVariable.swift](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo/blob/main/Sources/App/WeatherNext/WeatherNextVariable.swift)). Blend weights must therefore be learned per variable and lead. Equal weights would let the AI models' rain underprediction drag down totals.

NBM is the free US analogue of what the app is building. It bias-corrects each input with a decaying average and/or quantile mapping, weights inputs by "dynamic decaying MAEs and static expert weights" ([NOMADS BLEND](https://nomads.ncep.noaa.gov/txt_descriptions/BLEND_txt.html)), and uses gamma quantile mapping for precipitation ([NBM documentation](https://blend.mdl.nws.noaa.gov/nbm-documentation)). Version 5.0, effective 2026-04-30, moved several temperature and humidity variables to quantile mapping ([SCN 26-24](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-24_Updated_NBM_V5.0_aac.pdf)). At Colorado Basin sites a locally bias-corrected NBM was the best performer on MAE, while GFS MOS "generally… has the largest errors" ([CBRFC](https://www.cbrfc.noaa.gov/present/projects/NBM_Temperature.pdf)). At the extreme Mount Washington summit, NBM lagged MOS on wind speed ([KMWN verification](https://mountwashington.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/KMWN_MOS_Verification.pdf)).

HRRR handles light-to-moderate precipitation thresholds well ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10389149-evaluation-surface-wind-gust-forecasts-from-high-resolution-rapid-refresh-model)), so it belongs in the blend at 0–18 h, or 0–48 h for its longer runs. The regional model landscape is about to change. **RRFS goes operational on 2026-11-03**, retiring NAM, HREF, SREF and HiresW, while HRRR continues ([NWS SCN 26-48](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-048_Updated_RRFS_and_REFS_Implementation.aaf.pdf)). Open-Meteo has already built `ncep_rrfs_conus` ingestion but has not yet listed it publicly ([open-meteo RRFS README](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo/blob/main/docs/ncep-rrfs/README.md)).

### Post-processing: spread and rain probability are the untapped gains

The app's additive bias correction already captures most of the error in the forecast's central value. What the literature adds is calibration of the whole distribution. EMOS cut 2 m temperature CRPS by about 13% over the raw ensemble, and adding local station information gained roughly another 10% ([Rasp & Lerch, arXiv 1805.09091](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.09091)). Against an already bias-corrected ensemble, the original EMOS gained 7–9% in deterministic error and produced "much better calibrated" distributions ([Gneiting et al. 2005](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/gneiting2005.pdf)). This gain has not eroded as models improved: the advantage of post-processing "remains almost constant over time" from 2002 to 2014 ([Hemri et al. 2014](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/14547)).

Gusts gain the most. Simple post-processing that uses only the ensemble gust as input improved CRPS by up to **40%** over raw output ([EGU21-1326](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU21/EGU21-1326.html)). Neural networks then did better still in winter storms ([Schulz & Lerch 2022](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/schulz-lerch-machinelearningmethodsforpostprocessingensembleforecastsofwindgustsasystematiccomparison-2022)).

Neural networks with station embeddings beat EMOS by about 19% CRPS. But that result came from one model trained across 500+ German stations for a year ([Rasp & Lerch](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.09091)). On EUPPBench, which uses 20 years of reforecasts, "most postprocessing methods perform similarly" ([ESSD discussion](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/15/2635/2023/essd-15-2635-2023-discussion.html)). Training length matters more than adapting quickly: "reducing the variance… due to increased training data appears to be more important" than tracking the most recent data ([Lang et al. 2020](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000117421)). NAEFS's 2% decaying average, about 50–60 days of memory, improved all measures out to about day 5 ([EMC, Bo Cui](https://www.emc.ncep.noaa.gov/gmb/ens/Predictability_5Mar_BoCui.pdf)).

## Free data sources and what the most accurate apps actually do

**Open-Meteo** is the hub. Its free non-commercial tier offers HRRR (`ncep_hrrr_conus`, 3 km, hourly, with a 15-minute variant) and NBM (`ncep_nbm_conus`, 2.5 km, 11 days, hourly updates). It also offers AIFS Single (`ecmwf_aifs025_single`), AIGFS (`ncep_aigfs025`), ECMWF IFS HRES (`ecmwf_ifs`, 9 km), UKMO, WeatherNext 2 and the Previous Runs API ([docs/options.ts](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/options.ts); [GFS API docs source](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/gfs-api/+page.svelte)). Model identifiers are migrating to provider-prefixed names (`ncep_gfs_seamless`, `dwd_icon_seamless`, `cmc_gem_seamless`), while the legacy names still work ([ForecastapiController.swift](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo/blob/main/Sources/App/Controllers/ForecastapiController.swift)). Ensemble requests are charged per member, so ensemble means are far cheaper ([api-rate-limiting.md](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo/blob/main/docs/api-rate-limiting.md)).

Several sources complement it, and none needs an account. NOAA's real-time and un-restricted mesoscale analyses (RTMA/URMA) are hourly gridded "truth" fields, on AWS as `noaa-rtma-pds` and `noaa-urma-pds` ([AWS Registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/noaa-rtma)). NBM GRIB2 and station text bulletins are on AWS as `noaa-nbm-grib2-pds` ([AWS Registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/noaa-nbm); [MDL NBM download](https://vlab.noaa.gov/web/mdl/nbm-download)). The Herbie Python package reads HRRR, NBM and RTMA ([Herbie](https://herbie.readthedocs.io/en/latest/gallery/noaa_models/nbm.html)). The MRMS radar bucket (`noaa-mrms-pds`), Iowa Environmental Mesonet observation archives and the api.weather.gov latest-observation endpoint are standard choices, but none was verified in this research.

On commercial accuracy, ForecastWatch's own analysis named **Microsoft the most accurate global 1–5 day provider for the third straight year**, at 79.51% ([ForecastWatch](https://forecastwatch.com/2025/07/31/new-report-analysis-of-2024-one-to-five-day-out-forecasts/)). Microsoft also won US consumer days 1–7 in the 2026 awards, with Foreca winning days 8–14 ([ForecastWatch awards](https://forecastwatch.com/awards/2026/consumer-weather-north-america-united-states-all-metrics-8-14/)). The Weather Company and AccuWeather each promote ForecastWatch reports, some commissioned by the vendor, that name them as leaders ([TWC](https://www.weathercompany.com/proven-accuracy/); [AccuWeather](https://accuweather.com/en/weather-news/forecastwatch-releases-new-global-report-recognizing-accuweather-for-its-accuracy-leadership/83510)). Microsoft describes combining massive weather datasets, large-scale models, topography and real-time user reports ([Windows blog](https://blogs.windows.com/windowsexperience/2024/05/28/weather-from-microsoft-start-ai-powered-precision-in-forecasting/)). The shared recipe is many-model blending, ML post-processing against dense observations, and frequent updates. The app has now copied the first and third, so the gap that remains is the second.

## Audit of these findings

**Claims resting only on search snippets or abstracts.** Each research session had page fetches blocked for weather.gov, arxiv.org, ecmwf.int, forecastwatch.com and others. As a result, several load-bearing figures were seen only as search summaries:

- the LAMP 1–12 h result and its convergence to MOS ([Rudack 2008](https://www.weather.gov/media/mdl/Rudack2008.pdf)), and the LAMP/persistence equality at 1 h;
- the ECMWF-versus-persistence result;
- the pySTEPS "2 hours" figure;
- all post-processing CRPS numbers (Rasp & Lerch, Gneiting, Hemri, the 40% gust figure);
- ECMWF's AIFS "superior skill" statement and the AIFS ENS precipitation underprediction;
- the NOAA AIGFS and HGEFS claims;
- all ForecastWatch rankings.

The full ForecastWatch reports could not be read, so per-metric US rankings and the placement of Tomorrow.io, Apple and AccuWeather are unknown. The HRRR wind-gust and precipitation attribution is flagged in the notes as uncertain. The WeatherNext 2 performance claims come from secondary outlets, and the GenCast heavy-rain weaknesses come from a secondary summary, not the primary paper.

**Claims that could not be verified at all.** The following were not verified and should be checked before relying on them:

- the MRMS AWS bucket name and cadence;
- NOMADS rate limits and the AWS HRRR bucket;
- whether each AI model actually returns `_previous_dayN` values on Open-Meteo, and from what start date;
- whether RRFS will be publicly enabled on Open-Meteo.

Three quantities were not found anywhere: a lead-by-lead MAE table for LAMP against MOS, a US radar-versus-HRRR crossover hour, and any published fade time for an observation nudge. No source quantified wind-direction-conditioned bias correction at desert or southern-Nevada sites. No source quantified ML gains with only 3–6 months of data, and none quantified Brier-score gains of logistic PoP over member-count PoP.

**Where sources disagree.** Open-Meteo's blog describes its HRRR 15-minute data as 1 km resolution, while NOAA documents HRRR at 3 km ([Open-Meteo blog](https://openmeteo.substack.com/p/sub-hourly-15-minutely-weather-forecasts); [NOAA EMC](https://emc.ncep.noaa.gov/emc/pages/numerical_forecast_systems/hrrr.php)). GraphCast's headline is 90.0% versus 89.3% of targets beaten, and DGMR's forecaster preference is variously 89% or 93%. AIFS precipitation skill is reported as worse than IFS at short range in the original paper but "significantly improved" in the 2025 update. ECMWF also reports that the IFS 49r1 upgrade narrowed AIFS's 2 m temperature lead. Commercial "most accurate" claims conflict outright: Microsoft, TWC and AccuWeather each cite ForecastWatch, and several of those reports were commissioned by the vendor named. NBM beat MOS on temperature in Colorado but lagged it on wind at Mount Washington. That is more likely site dependence than a contradiction.

**Inference rather than evidence.** The following rest on reasoning rather than on any study:

- The fade-time ranges (temperature 3–6 h, wind 1–3 h) and the advice to cap the correction and skip it at fronts or rain onset. These are reasoning, not data.
- The claim that upwind-station reading adds little beyond the next model cycle. This extrapolates from ECMWF's observation-impact data and HRRR's hourly assimilation, not from a direct test.
- All mechanisms in the weather-pattern section: inversions under highs, downslope warming, marine layer, monsoon cloud, and the scale argument for a pressure centre 160 km away.
- The view that radar adds less in the US because HRRR assimilates radar.
- The idea of training a pooled model on non-display ASOS stations.
- The recommendations for logistic PoP, multiplicative wind correction and LightGBM. These follow standard practice in the field, but no source here measured them for an app like this one.
- The characterisation of what commercial winners do, which is inferred from marketing descriptions.

## Conclusion and ranked implementation plan

The research changes where effort should go. The intuitive upgrades (watching upwind stations, reading radar, reacting to the latest observation) live inside a window of a few hours that HRRR already serves well. The durable gains from 6 hours to 7 days come from statistics: correcting bias by regime and lead, calibrating spread and rain probability, and weighting partly independent models per variable. The app's new 365-day archive is the enabling asset. Most of the methods that need about a year of data, such as analog regimes, seasonal fits and pooled calibration, become viable only as it fills. The 2% cross-validation gate should therefore be expected to switch on more regime corrections over the coming months. The backtester (f) should score CRPS, Brier score and reliability, not just MAE, because the next wins are in calibration rather than in the central forecast.
